The Problem You're Solving
You signed up for the hackathon. Now you're staring at the confirmation email wondering what exactly you're supposed to do next. There's a platform to join, a team to find, challenges to read up on, tools to set up, and a submission to prepare, all within a four-day window spanning multiple time zones.

Most first-time hackathon participants lose hours just figuring out the basics. They miss key announcements, scramble to find teammates at the last minute, or realize too late that their submission is missing a required component.

This guide walks you through everything you need to do before, during, and after the hackathon so you can spend your time building, not troubleshooting.

Time: ~30 minutes to set up Level: All levels Tools: GitHub (optional)

Step 1: Get Set Up on the Workspace
Before anything else, join the Slack community space. This is where every important announcement, team formation, mentor connection, and submission happens. If you're not there, you'll miss it.

Check your registration confirmation email for the invite link. Once inside, complete your profile with your full name, a headshot, a short bio covering your background and skills, and whether you're looking for a team or already have one. A complete profile is the difference between strangers reaching out to you and nobody knowing you exist.

Key channels to follow immediately:

The channels you need to pay attention to are #announcements (check at least twice a day), #introduce_yourself, #find_a_team, #find_a_mentor, #ask_the_hackathon_team, and #resources. The #register_your_team & submit channel is where you'll handle all official team and submission forms.

Step 2: Find Your Team
You don't need a team to register, and you don't need to have one before the event starts. Many of the best teams form organically in the days leading up to the hackathon.

There are three ways to find teammates. You can recruit from your existing network of classmates or colleagues. You can post in the #find_a_team channel and introduce yourself, your skills, and the challenge you're interested in. Or you can join one of the speed-teaming sessions scheduled before the hackathon kicks off, which are the most efficient way to meet a lot of people quickly.

When posting to find a team, be specific. "I'm a frontend developer interested in Challenge 2, looking for a data scientist and someone with business modelling experience" will get you further than "Looking for a team." The recommended team size is 2 to 6 members, with a maximum of 8.

What you'll get:

Once your team is set, register it using the form in #register_your_team & submit. You can update team members later if needed by re-submitting the form. The most recent submission is always the one on record.

Step 3: Choose Your Challenge and Find a Mentor
The hackathon runs four challenge categories: Civic Access & Community Communication, Workforce, Business & Economic Growth, Smart Cities, Infrastructure & Public Spaces, and Public Safety, Emergency Response & City Analytics. Spend time on the landing page reading the context for each before the event starts. You don't need to commit immediately, but knowing which direction you're leaning lets you find the right mentor.

Mentors are one of the most underused resources in any hackathon. They can save you hours of dead ends and help you pressure-test whether your idea is feasible before you've invested too much time in it. The ideal setup is three mentors per team: one with business knowledge, one with technical expertise, and one subject matter expert in your challenge area.

To find a mentor, post in #ask_a_mentor or respond to mentor introductions in that channel. You can also reach out to specific mentors directly once the event begins. Mentors will approach teams too, but don't wait to be found. Reach out early, pick mentors whose time zones overlap with your team's working hours, and have more than one so you're never stuck waiting.

Step 4: Prepare Before the Hackathon Starts
The teams that do best are the ones who arrive ready. Preparation happens in two areas: logistics and research.

On the logistics side, set up your GitHub repository now, even if you haven't settled on an idea. Familiarize yourself with the workspace channels. Make sure your hardware is ready (chargers, headphones, a stable internet connection). Read the rules and code of conduct so nothing surprises you during the event.

On the research side, read the challenge descriptions carefully. Look at what solutions already exist in those spaces. Think about the skill set you bring and what gaps your team needs to fill. The primer pack available in #hackathon_resources_hub has ideation, presentation, and project management templates that are genuinely useful.

What to do if things go wrong during the event:

If your mentor isn't responding, post in #ask_a_mentor. The whole mentor community can help, not just your assigned one. If a team member goes quiet, talk to your mentor, then post in #find_a_team if you need to recruit someone new. If you're confused about the rules, go to #ask_hackathon_team. These channels exist precisely because these situations are common.

Step 5: Build and Submit
The hackathon runs from Friday 9pm EST to Monday 9pm EST. Use that time window intentionally. The Friday evening and Monday morning slots are there specifically for people balancing the event with work or other commitments.

Submissions require three components. A video (2 to 4 minutes, hosted publicly on YouTube or another platform) is the primary assessment item. A document of up to 8 pages describing your solution, in Word, Google Docs, or PDF, is compulsory. A GitHub or other code repository link is highly recommended.

Submit through #register_your_team & submit. If you miss the deadline, late submissions are accepted but incur a penalty of 1 point for every 2-hour window past the deadline, up to a maximum of 5 points deducted.

What you'll get:

Your submission will be reviewed by organizers and, if shortlisted, passed to judges for scoring across four criteria: consistency with the challenge statement (15 points), quality and design (10 points), originality and impact (10 points), and commercialisation potential (5 points). Bonus points are available for using sponsored technologies.

What You've Built
By completing this process, you have:

A registered team with clearly defined roles, a chosen challenge, and at least one mentor lined up before the event starts.

A prepared workspace setup so you never miss an announcement or scramble to find a channel mid-hackathon.

A submission-ready workflow where you know exactly what's needed, what the deadline is, and what the penalty structure looks like.

A support network of mentors, organizers, and fellow participants you can actually reach when things get difficult.

Ideas to Get You Started
If you don't have an idea yet: Start by posting in #introduce_yourself and reading what others are interested in. Ideas emerge from conversation. You can also join someone else's idea if it resonates with you more than anything you'd come up with on your own.

If you're a solo participant: Individual submissions are accepted. You can still use mentors and the community for feedback. Some of the most focused submissions come from individuals who didn't have to manage team coordination.

If you're worried about your technical skills: Hackathons are built for mixed-skill teams. Non-technical contributions like user research, business modelling, presentation design, and project management have won competitions. What matters is that your team covers the bases together.

If you want to use sponsored tools: Check the landing page for sponsor offerings. Using any sponsored technology gives you 2 bonus points in that sponsor's challenge category, or 1 bonus point if used in a different category. Points can stack across multiple sponsors.

If you're competing across time zones: Plan explicitly for async work. Decide in your first team meeting which hours each member is available and how you'll hand off work across time zones. The Friday and Monday slots exist for exactly this reason.

If your team loses a member mid-hackathon: Post in #find_a_team. It happens more often than people expect. Other participants drop out or are looking to switch teams. The organizers can also help with matching.

A Note on Winning vs. Learning
It's easy to get fixated on the prize and forget why hackathons are worth doing regardless of the outcome. The teams that go furthest are usually the ones who stay curious rather than competitive, who treat mentor feedback as useful rather than threatening, and who build something they'd actually want to use rather than something they think judges want to see.

The judging criteria reward originality and real-world impact. A polished, incremental idea tends to score lower than a raw but genuinely novel approach with a clear use case. Don't water down your concept to make it feel safer. Push the idea as far as you can in the time you have.

The submission is a snapshot of four days of work. It won't be perfect. What it needs to be is clear, demonstrable, and honest about what it is and what it could become.

The Problem You're Solving
You've agreed to judge. You've been sent a link to a spreadsheet and told you'll have 10 to 15 projects to assess. The projects are diverse, the teams are from different backgrounds, and you have limited time to form a fair view of work that spans technical execution, business thinking, and creative problem-solving.

Judging a hackathon without a clear process produces inconsistent outcomes. You might overweight the first video you watch, underweight something that starts slowly but is genuinely novel, or feel uncertain about how to score areas outside your expertise. The result is rankings that don't reflect the actual quality of the work.

This guide gives you a structured process for assessing submissions fairly and efficiently, so your scores reflect what you actually think, not what order you watched the videos in.

Time: 1 to 2 hours total Level: Experienced professionals Tools: Judging spreadsheet (emailed after hackathon), YouTube or video platform

Step 1: Understand What You're Evaluating
Before you watch a single video, get clear on the two types of submissions you'll see and what success looks like for each.

Option 1: Production-ready solution. This is a working implementation with immediate practical value. Think a functioning dashboard, a predictive algorithm, or a deployable tool. Assess it on whether it actually works, whether the code quality holds up, and whether it solves a real problem.

Option 2: Proof of concept with strong potential. This is a compelling vision backed by evidence of feasibility. Think a pitch deck with commercial modelling, an economic simulation, or a clearly reasoned technical architecture. Assess it on clarity of thinking, quality of evidence, and plausibility of the pathway to impact.

Both types are eligible. Neither is inherently superior. A brilliant proof of concept with rigorous commercial thinking can score higher than a working prototype with no clear use case.


Step 2: Know the Scoring Criteria
All submissions are scored out of 40 points across four criteria. Read these before you start watching videos so you're evaluating actively rather than reacting.

Consistency with challenge statements (15 points): How directly does the solution address the stated problem? Does it stay within scope? A solution that's technically impressive but tangentially related to the challenge should score lower here.

Quality and design (10 points): For technical submissions, this means code quality and architecture, assessed via the GitHub or code repository link. For design-led submissions, this means the clarity and rigour of the design thinking.

Originality and impact (10 points): How different is this from what already exists? How meaningful could the impact be if it reached the intended users or context at scale?

Commercialisation potential (5 points): Is there a plausible business model or pathway to sustained operation? This doesn't require a full financial model, but teams should have thought about how this continues to exist beyond the hackathon.

Bonus points: Using sponsored technologies earns teams additional points, which have already been factored into the scoring sheet you'll receive.

Step 3: Work Through Submissions Systematically
The most reliable judging process separates initial screening from detailed assessment. Here's the sequence that works.

First pass: read the problem statements. Before watching any videos, read each submission's written document (up to 8 pages). This gives you a baseline sense of what each team is trying to solve and lets you watch the videos with context rather than forming impressions cold.

Second pass: watch the videos. Each video is 2 to 4 minutes. Watch all of them before assigning any scores. After watching, note your top three to five candidates based on problem relevance and how well the video communicates the solution. Don't finalise rankings yet.

Third pass: assess the detailed criteria. For your top candidates, go deeper. Review the GitHub repository for code quality and documentation. Re-read the document for commercial thinking and originality evidence. This is where your initial impressions get tested against the actual work.

Fourth pass: assign scores and submit. Locate your row in the scoring sheet and enter numerical scores for each criterion. Add comments where they'd be useful for the organisers. Submit before the deadline specified in the email.


Step 4: Handle Areas Outside Your Expertise
You don't need to have an opinion on every criterion for every submission. The scoring system is designed to aggregate across multiple judges, so gaps in individual expertise average out.

If you genuinely don't have the background to assess a particular criterion, for example, if you have no software engineering experience and the quality of a codebase is beyond your ability to evaluate, leave that field blank. Your input on the criteria where you do have expertise is more valuable than a guess on the ones where you don't.

What you should avoid is over-indexing on your areas of expertise at the expense of the others. A submission that's technically brilliant but completely misses the challenge statement should not score highly overall just because one judge happens to specialise in the technology used.

Contributed directly to fair outcomes for teams who spent four days building something ambitious under significant time pressure.

Applied your domain expertise to evaluate work at the intersection of AI, social impact, and commercial viability, an increasingly relevant space across most professional fields.

A basis for connecting with teams and other judges whose work caught your attention during the assessment process.

Visibility within the GenAI Works community, with recognition as part of the official judging panel for a global hackathon.

Ideas to Get You Started
Before you start: Scan all the problem statements first as a batch. This helps you calibrate the range of ambition and scope across submissions before any one video anchors your expectations.

For videos that start slowly: Don't form a strong opinion in the first 30 seconds. Some of the most substantive submissions front-load context rather than impact. Watch the full video before deciding how you feel about it.

For submissions with impressive demos but weak problem framing: A working tool that doesn't address a real need is less valuable than a well-reasoned approach to a genuine problem. The challenge statement criterion exists precisely to prevent technical polish from substituting for relevance.

For originality assessment: Ask yourself whether the core insight behind the solution is something you've seen before. The technology used matters less than whether the application is genuinely new. Applying an existing technique to an overlooked context can be highly original.

For commercial potential: You're not assessing whether you personally would invest in this. You're assessing whether the team has demonstrated awareness of how this could sustain itself, whether through revenue, partnership, or institutional adoption.

A Note on What Hackathon Judging Actually Measures
Hackathon submissions are four days of work under significant constraints. What you're assessing is not a finished product. It's the quality of thinking under pressure, the clarity of problem framing, the intelligence of the technical or design choices made with limited time and resources, and the plausibility of the vision.

Some of the strongest submissions will have rough edges. Some of the most polished submissions will be shallow on impact. Your job is to distinguish between those two things and reward the substance.

The video is the primary artifact because most judges don't have time to assess every line of code or read every page of every document. But for submissions where the video is compelling, the additional materials are worth reviewing carefully. A team that submits strong code alongside a strong video has demonstrated something more than a team that produces one without the other.

Judging is inherently subjective. The process described here doesn't eliminate that, but it structures it. Following the sequence produces more consistent scores than forming impressions ad hoc, and more consistent scores produce fairer outcomes for the teams.
